## Overview

This post presents evidence of my participation in **Collaborative Learning Discussion 1**. The discussion focused on Hancock et al. (2024) and the risks that data and digital technologies may introduce or aggravate in human rights investigations. The activity required students to consider how technology can affect fairness and accountability, especially when digital systems are developed without direct input from the communities they are intended to protect.

This topic is relevant to Security and Risk Management because digital tools can support investigations, evidence collection and accountability. However, they can also introduce risks linked to bias, exclusion, surveillance, privacy, data misuse and weak governance.

---

## Initial Post Summary

My initial post argued that data and digital technologies can strengthen human rights investigations by expanding access to evidence and making less visible harms easier to identify. However, the post also highlighted that these tools can create risks to fairness and accountability when they are treated as neutral or purely technical solutions.

The post identified several risks. First, datasets may be incomplete, unevenly produced or shaped by existing inequalities. This can result in the experiences of marginalised communities being under-represented or excluded. Second, accountability may be weakened when digital systems make it difficult to trace responsibility for errors or decisions. Third, technologies developed without community input may be inaccessible, unsafe or misaligned with local priorities.

The post concluded that mitigation should begin during the design phase. This includes participatory risk assessment, privacy-by-design, accessible interfaces, community testing, human review and clear accountability mechanisms.

## Key Themes from the Discussion

The discussion highlighted four important themes. First, digital technology can support human rights investigations, but it should not be treated as automatically neutral or objective. Second, fairness depends on the quality, completeness and context of the data being used. Third, accountability requires clear governance, audit trails, explainability and human review. Finally, affected communities should be involved early in the design process because they understand local risks, safety concerns and practical barriers better than external designers.

---

## Summary Post

The peer contributions provided valuable insights that helped extend the discussion by highlighting additional considerations around fairness, accountability, surveillance risks and data protection. The discussion has shown that data and digital technologies can support human rights investigations, but they may also create risks to fairness, accountability and safety if they are not designed and governed carefully. The initial post argued that digital tools should not be treated as neutral solutions, because incomplete datasets, unequal access to reporting mechanisms and limited community participation can reproduce existing inequalities. Hancock et al. (2024) demonstrate this concern in the context of maritime human rights issues, where data and technology may help identify hidden harms, but may also exclude affected groups if evidence is shaped by unequal visibility or external assumptions.

Payman highlighted the importance of data quality assessment, bias detection, audit trails and governance frameworks. This reinforces the argument that fairness requires more than data collection; it requires systemic validation and clear responsibility for how evidence is interpreted and applied. Sèba added a further dimension by focusing on surveillance and retaliation risks. This is particularly significant in human rights investigations, where victims, witnesses and community informants may face harm if sensitive information is stored, accessed or transmitted insecurely. Therefore, threat modelling and adversary-aware risk assessment should be included before deployment, especially where state-level or powerful actors may be involved. Sopheaktra further strengthened the discussion by emphasising data minimisation, socio-technical security and digital activity logs as mechanisms for improving both protection and accountability.

Overall, the discussion across Units 1, 2 and 3 indicates that responsible technology use in human rights investigations requires a balanced socio-technical approach. Digital evidence should be verified, contextualised and preserved rather than accepted as objective by default. Furthermore, affected communities should participate meaningfully in the design phase, not only as consultees but as stakeholders whose safety, priorities and lived experiences shape the system. Technology can therefore strengthen human rights investigations only when fairness, accountability, privacy, security and community agency are built into the design process from the beginning.

## Reflection

This activity improved my understanding of the relationship between digital technology, fairness and accountability in human rights investigations. Before the discussion, my main focus was on incomplete data, community exclusion and weak accountability. However, the peer responses helped me see that the issue is wider than data quality alone.

The discussion showed that digital systems may also create surveillance, retaliation and privacy risks if sensitive information is not protected. It also highlighted that accountability depends on governance, audit trails, explainability and human review. This helped me understand that responsible technology design requires both technical safeguards and social safeguards.

Overall, the activity strengthened my ability to evaluate digital risk from a socio-technical perspective. It showed that technology should support human judgement and community agency rather than replacing them. The most important learning point was that affected communities should be involved from the beginning of the design process, because fairness and safety cannot be added only after deployment.
