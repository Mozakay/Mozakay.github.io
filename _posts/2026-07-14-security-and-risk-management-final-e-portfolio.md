## Portfolio Overview

This e-portfolio consolidates evidence of academic, technical and collaborative development across the Security and Risk Management module. It presents at least one artefact from each unit, identifies the student’s individual contribution to group and individual activities, evaluates the progression from the Unit 6 status document to the Unit 11 final project, and provides a critical reflection on the security and risk management process, teamwork and professional development.

The portfolio demonstrates a progression from examining social, ethical and legal risks to applying qualitative and quantitative risk assessment methods. It also shows the development of practical skills in threat modelling, Bayesian reasoning, Monte Carlo simulation, vulnerability prioritisation, disaster recovery design and the assessment of emerging risks. Where one activity extended across several units, the initial contribution, peer engagement and final synthesis are presented as separate stages of learning.

## Evaluation of Unit 11 Against Unit 6

The Unit 6 status document used an asset-based qualitative method aligned with NIST CSF 2.0. It was appropriate for a small organisation with limited historical data because it linked assets, threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood, impact and mitigation in a form that could be understood by non-specialist decision-makers (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024). However, qualitative ratings can conceal uncertainty and may create the appearance of precision where evidence is limited.

By contrast, the Unit 11 project treated the business as an interconnected digital supply-chain system. It distinguished product, information and financial flows and modelled five risks that could affect quality, availability and security. Triangular probability distributions and 10,000 Monte Carlo iterations were used to represent uncertainty, while exposure scores supported prioritisation. This extended the Unit 7 simulation learning and reflected the wider use of simulation for supply-chain disruption analysis (Schmitt and Singh, 2009). Figure 6 shows the resulting probability estimates, and Figure 7 presents the active-active multi-region disaster recovery design.

This reflects the principle that quantitative outputs should support, rather than substitute for, professional judgement.

## Final Critical Reflection

The final reflection follows Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper’s (2001) **What? So What? Now What?** structure.

### What?

I began the module with a mainly control-focused understanding of security and risk management. Risks were viewed as problems that could be identified, assigned likelihood and impact ratings, and matched to controls. The early discussion on human rights investigations challenged this position by showing that digital risk also concerns fairness, accountability and community participation. Peer responses widened the analysis to include surveillance, retaliation and physical-safety risks. This demonstrated that a technically functional system may still create ethical or social harm.

Unit 4 introduced a more structured view of assets and interdependencies. Jbair et al. (2022) showed that cyber-physical risk cannot be understood by examining components in isolation because weaknesses can create cascading operational and safety effects. Unit 5 added a legal dimension through the University of Limerick case, where phishing, weak account security and delayed breach management produced both technical and regulatory consequences (Data Protection Commission, 2025).

The Unit 6 team project provided the first opportunity to apply these ideas to a complete business scenario. My principal contribution was the current-state risk assessment, together with source identification, integration and final review. Later activities introduced Bayesian updating, Monte Carlo simulation, social-engineering models, vulnerability prioritisation and disaster recovery. These activities culminated in the Unit 11 individual project and Unit 12 quantum-computing debate.

### So What?

The most important change was recognising that risk assessment is not a single calculation. It is an iterative process shaped by scope, assumptions, evidence, stakeholders and business consequences. NIST CSF 2.0 structured the Unit 6 assessment through Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover; however, the later work showed that a framework does not remove uncertainty (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024). It provides a disciplined structure within which judgement must be explained.

The probability activities were initially challenging because numerical results can appear more authoritative than their supporting assumptions. The Think Bayes exercises demonstrated that conditional probability changes with the direction of the condition and that evidence must be interpreted in relation to the process that generated it (Downey, 2022). Monte Carlo simulation similarly produces a distribution of plausible outcomes rather than a guaranteed prediction (Metropolis and Ulam, 1949). This became important in Unit 11 because historical data did not exist for the proposed international digital supply chain. Precise frequencies could not be defended, so probability ranges, documented assumptions and a deterministic cross-check were used. I was initially concerned that the estimates might appear subjective. However, that concern encouraged more careful source selection, clearer limitations and a stronger distinction between decision support and prediction.

The comparison between Units 6 and 11 changed my understanding of methodological quality. The Unit 6 qualitative tables were accessible and proportionate for initial prioritisation, but they compressed several judgements into Low, Medium and High categories. Unit 11 represented uncertainty more transparently, although it introduced limitations such as approximate independence and scenario-based estimates. The lesson was not that quantitative analysis is automatically superior. The selected method must be proportionate to the decision, available evidence and consequences of error.

Peer engagement directly improved the reasoning. In the CVSS discussion, the initial position favoured SSVC as a replacement for CVSS. Peer comments questioned whether complete replacement would reduce consistency between vendors, regulators and security teams. This led to a more balanced conclusion: CVSS remains useful for severity communication, while SSVC provides a more contextual basis for prioritisation (Wunder et al., 2024; Spring et al., 2020). The experience demonstrated that critical thinking requires a willingness to revise an argument when stronger reasoning becomes available.

The team activities also revealed strengths and limitations in collaborative practice. Dividing the Unit 6 work supported efficient progress, and the early meeting clarified scope and deadlines. However, task division can create inconsistent terminology, assumptions and levels of detail. Integration and final review were therefore as important as initial drafting. Unit 12 reinforced this lesson because a persuasive debate required one continuous argument rather than several unrelated slide sets. Figure 8 evidences task allocation, but the quality of the result depended on maintaining a consistent group position.

Professionally, the module strengthened the ability to connect technical controls with legal, operational and strategic requirements. The GDPR case showed that authentication, monitoring and incident response are also mechanisms of accountability. The disaster recovery activities demonstrated that RTO and RPO influence architecture, testing and supplier decisions rather than operating as abstract policy statements. The quantum-computing debate further showed that risk professionals may need to prepare before likelihood can be estimated confidently. Long-term confidentiality and migration lead times justify early cryptographic inventory and crypto-agility planning despite uncertainty about the arrival of a cryptographically relevant quantum computer (National Cyber Security Centre, 2025).

### Now What?

Future assessments will begin with a clear definition of the system, stakeholders, assets, dependencies and decision purpose. Qualitative analysis will support initial scoping, followed by quantitative modelling where evidence and decision significance justify it. Probability ranges, sensitivity analysis and dependencies between risks will be examined explicitly. Initial estimates will be updated when operational evidence becomes available.

In team activities, roles, terminology and evidence requirements will be agreed at the outset. A shared evidence matrix, version control and scheduled integration reviews will be used to reduce duplication and inconsistency. Peer feedback will be sought before final drafting so that it can shape the argument.

In professional practice, the learning will be applied to supplier assurance, vulnerability management, data protection, disaster recovery testing and emerging-technology governance. Personal development will focus on concise analytical writing, recording assumptions when decisions are made and maintaining a reference audit. Overall, the module changed my approach from identifying isolated threats to evaluating interconnected socio-technical systems. Transparent assumptions, structured analysis, critical dialogue and tested controls now form the basis of how I would support responsible risk decisions.

## Conclusion

The portfolio demonstrates development across all twelve units through linked artefacts, individual and collaborative evidence, methodological comparison and critical reflection. The progression from qualitative risk identification to quantitative modelling and disaster recovery design shows increased analytical depth, while the discussion activities demonstrate that peer engagement contributed to revised and more balanced conclusions. The module also established that security and risk management requires integration of technical, legal, ethical, operational and professional considerations.
