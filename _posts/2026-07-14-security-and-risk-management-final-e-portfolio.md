## Portfolio Overview

This e-portfolio critically evaluates how understanding of security and risk management developed across the module. The evidence is organised by changes in reasoning rather than by a chronological list of completed units. It demonstrates movement from identifying isolated technical threats towards evaluating interconnected socio-technical systems, from using broad qualitative ratings towards representing uncertainty explicitly, and from viewing recovery as backup restoration towards understanding operational resilience, dependency and governance.

The portfolio also evaluates individual and collaborative contributions. Particular attention is given to the progression from the qualitative *Security Risk Identification Report: Pampered Pets* to the quantitative *Post-Digitalisation Risk Assessment and Disaster Recovery Strategy*. This comparison provides the strongest evidence of increased analytical independence, more transparent treatment of assumptions and stronger integration between security, law, business continuity and operational priorities.

## Critical Development Evidenced by the Artefacts

The initial [human-rights discussion]({% post_url 2026-05-05-collaborative-learning-discussion-1-human-rights-technology %}) challenged a mainly technical interpretation of risk. Digital systems were shown to create exclusion, surveillance, privacy and safety concerns even when they operated as designed. Peer engagement extended the initial analysis from data quality and fairness to retaliation, data minimisation, auditability and community protection. The subsequent synthesis demonstrated that risk assessment should examine who may be harmed, whose evidence is represented and how accountability is maintained.

The [Industrial Cyber-Physical Systems activity]({% post_url 2026-05-26-risk-identification-and-modelling %}) developed this reasoning further. Jbair et al. (2022) showed that assets cannot always be assessed in isolation because vulnerabilities may create cascading consequences across digital systems, physical processes, safety and production continuity. The [University of Limerick GDPR case]({% post_url 2026-05-23-gdpr-case-study-university-of-limerick %}) added a legal and governance dimension: phishing and weak account security were not only technical failures, since delayed detection, incomplete records and failures in breach notification also created regulatory and accountability consequences (Data Protection Commission, 2025).

The [Pampered Pets team assessment]({% post_url 2026-06-06-pampered-pets-risk-identification-report %}) applied qualitative risk assessment using NIST CSF 2.0. Its Low, Medium and High ratings were accessible and proportionate to a small organisation, but they compressed evidence and judgement into broad categories. The [Think Bayes activity]({% post_url 2026-06-13-think-bayes-probability-and-bayesian-updating %}) then developed understanding of conditional probability and the revision of estimates when new evidence becomes available (Downey, 2022). The [Monte Carlo activity]({% post_url 2026-06-13-monte-carlo-simulation-for-risk-modelling %}) showed that uncertainty can be represented through repeated sampling and distributions rather than a single prediction (Metropolis and Ulam, 1949).

The [social-engineering modelling activity]({% post_url 2026-06-09-modelling-social-engineering-threats %}) distinguished Attack Occurrence Probability from Attack Success Probability. This demonstrated that a frequent attack may not be the most effective and that human behaviour cannot be represented fully through numerical values (Aijaz and Nazir, 2024). The [CVSS and SSVC discussion]({% post_url 2026-06-22-collaborative-learning-discussion-2-cvss-ssvc %}) similarly separated technical severity from contextual priority. Peer feedback changed an initially replacement-focused position into a balanced approach that retained CVSS for severity communication and used SSVC for risk-informed prioritisation (Spring et al., 2020; Wunder et al., 2024).

The [disaster recovery activity]({% post_url 2026-06-18-unit-10-dr-solutions-design-and-review %}) shifted the focus from backup availability to operational resilience. Recovery came to include RTO, RPO, portability, secure configuration, identity management, monitoring, vendor dependency and tested failover. These ideas were integrated into the [post-digitalisation individual assessment]({% post_url 2026-07-14-unit-11-post-digitalisation-risk-assessment %}), where probability ranges, 10,000 Monte Carlo iterations, a deterministic cross-check and an active-active multi-region design were applied. The [quantum-computing debate]({% post_url 2026-06-22-great-debate-quantum-computing %}) extended risk analysis to uncertain future threats, demonstrating that long migration lead times may justify early preparation even when immediate likelihood cannot be estimated confidently.

The evidence spans all twelve units without becoming a chronological checklist. The initial human-rights contribution, [peer responses]({% post_url 2026-05-05-collaborative-learning-discussion-1-human-rights-technology %}#peer-responses-received) and [final synthesis]({% post_url 2026-05-05-collaborative-learning-discussion-1-human-rights-technology %}#summary-post) demonstrate development across Units 1–3. Risk identification, the GDPR case and the Pampered Pets assessment provide evidence for Units 4–6. Think Bayes, Monte Carlo simulation, social-engineering modelling and the [CVSS/SSVC peer discussion and synthesis]({% post_url 2026-06-22-collaborative-learning-discussion-2-cvss-ssvc %}#summary-post) demonstrate development across Units 7–9. Disaster recovery design, the post-digitalisation individual assessment and the quantum-computing debate provide the principal evidence for Units 10–12. The linked artefacts contain discussion records, code, risk tables, meeting notes, presentation material and reflective evaluation.

## Individual Contribution and Collaboration

The principal contribution to the Pampered Pets team project was the Current Business Risk Assessment. It identified risks associated with ageing technology, shared networks, customer information and manual inventory processes, and also involved source identification, integration of recommendations and final review. Its value was that it established the baseline against which digitalisation could be evaluated. Its limitation was that some qualitative ratings required fuller justification and earlier documentation of assumptions. The linked [meeting notes and task-coordination evidence]({% post_url 2026-06-06-pampered-pets-risk-identification-report %}#collaboration-and-meeting-evidence) show that this contribution formed part of an agreed team process rather than an isolated section of work.

The later individual project demonstrated increased independence through academic research, [Python modelling and reproducibility evidence]({% post_url 2026-07-14-unit-11-post-digitalisation-risk-assessment %}#python-model-and-reproducibility-evidence), interpretation of aggregate outcomes, prioritised recommendations and disaster recovery design. The contribution to the quantum-computing presentation developed strategic communication by translating an uncertain technical issue into a coherent argument for a non-specialist audience. The [meeting and work-division evidence]({% post_url 2026-06-22-great-debate-quantum-computing %}#collaboration-and-preparation-evidence) demonstrates coordinated preparation and a defined individual role within the presentation.

Collaboration improved the work when it changed reasoning rather than merely divided tasks. The [human-rights peer responses]({% post_url 2026-05-05-collaborative-learning-discussion-1-human-rights-technology %}#peer-responses-received) broadened the analysis, while the [CVSS and SSVC peer evidence]({% post_url 2026-06-22-collaborative-learning-discussion-2-cvss-ssvc %}#peer-responses-received) materially revised the final conclusion. Team projects also showed that early allocation improves accountability, but separate drafting can create inconsistency in terminology, assumptions and referencing. Common standards and integration reviews were therefore necessary to produce a coherent outcome.

## Critical Evaluation of Unit 11 against Unit 6

The Unit 6 status document provided a proportionate qualitative baseline by linking Pampered Pets’ existing assets, threats and vulnerabilities to Low, Medium and High ratings. This method was accessible to a small organisation and supported prioritisation through NIST CSF 2.0. However, the categories compressed several judgements into a single label and did not represent the extent of uncertainty behind each likelihood assessment.

Unit 11 extended this foundation by treating the organisation as an interconnected digital supply-chain system rather than a collection of isolated assets. Probability ranges, triangular distributions and 10,000 Monte Carlo iterations represented uncertainty more explicitly, while a deterministic cross-check supported review of the model’s internal consistency. The analysis also moved from individual risk ratings to aggregate effects on product quality, availability and security. Disaster recovery requirements were translated into an active-active multi-region design with measurable RTO and RPO objectives.

The later project nevertheless remained dependent on scenario-based estimates, selected distributions and an approximate independence assumption. The progression from Unit 6 to Unit 11 therefore did not replace qualitative judgement with numerical certainty. Instead, it demonstrated a more transparent and independent approach in which qualitative scoping, quantitative modelling and professional judgement were combined according to the importance of the decision and the quality of the available evidence.

## Final Critical Reflection

### Introduction

Effective security and risk management require more than identifying threats and selecting controls; they depend on evidence quality, transparent assumptions and proportionate judgement. Across the module, understanding developed from a control-centred view into a broader appreciation of security as a socio-technical, legal and operational process. This reflection applies Rolfe, Freshwater and Jasper’s (2001) framework to evaluate risk understanding, individual contribution, collaboration, and professional and personal development.

### What?

Risk assessment was initially approached through assets, threats, vulnerabilities, likelihood, impact and mitigation. However, the human-rights discussion exposed the limits of a purely technical perspective by showing that digital systems may create exclusion, surveillance, privacy risks and harm. Peer contributions extended the analysis to retaliation, data minimisation and community protection. The analysis of Industrial Cyber-Physical Systems also showed how vulnerabilities may create cascading effects across connected technical and operational components, including safety and production continuity.

A more applied understanding emerged through the Pampered Pets project. The Current Business Risk Assessment involved identifying weaknesses in ageing technology, shared networks, customer records and manual inventory processes, reviewing sources and integrating recommendations. Think Bayes and Monte Carlo simulation later introduced probability-based reasoning. These activities informed the post-digitalisation assessment, while work on social engineering, vulnerability prioritisation, cloud recovery and quantum computing extended learning into human factors, resilience and strategic communication.

### So What?

The most significant change concerned the limitations of simple risk ratings. In the Pampered Pets assessment, qualitative categories supported clear communication and were proportionate to a small organisation. NIST CSF 2.0 also linked governance, protection, detection, response and recovery (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024). Nevertheless, labels such as Low, Medium and High can conceal the evidence and assumptions behind a rating. Qualitative assessment therefore became credible only when its reasoning was explicit.

Probability was initially understood mainly as a single estimate attached to a risk. Monte Carlo simulation demonstrated that uncertain inputs could instead be represented through ranges and distributions, producing plausible outcomes rather than one fixed result (Metropolis and Ulam, 1949). Think Bayes further showed that probability changes when evidence is introduced and depends on how hypotheses and likelihoods are defined (Downey, 2022). Numerical outputs may therefore appear precise while remaining dependent on assumptions, data quality and model design.

Social-engineering modelling showed that attack occurrence and attack success are not equivalent. Attack Tree and Markov Chain models distinguished how frequently an attack pattern may arise from how effectively it may progress to compromise (Aijaz and Nazir, 2024). However, human behaviour, organisational culture and interaction context cannot be represented fully through probability values. This reinforced the need to combine modelling with contextual judgement.

This issue became central in the post-digitalisation assessment. Historical data did not exist for the proposed international supply chain, so minimum, most likely and maximum estimates replaced unsupported exact frequencies. Triangular distributions, 10,000 iterations and a deterministic cross-check improved transparency, although the results remained sensitive to the selected ranges and approximate independence assumption. The absence of historical data created uncertainty about the credibility of scenario-based estimates, encouraging clearer documentation and greater caution when communicating findings. Quantitative assessment was therefore understood as decision support rather than prediction.

The complexity and independence of the individual contribution increased throughout the module. The Current Business Risk Assessment established a baseline for evaluating digitalisation and demonstrated strengths in research, organisation and evidence integration. However, some ratings required fuller justification, assumptions could have been recorded earlier and more time should have been reserved for critical review. The later project demonstrated greater independence through academic evidence, Python modelling, prioritisation and disaster recovery design.

Collaboration produced benefits and methodological challenges. Dividing the Pampered Pets project into defined sections improved accountability and reduced duplication, but separate drafting risked inconsistencies in terminology, structure and referencing. Integration was therefore essential. In the CVSS and SSVC discussion, an initial preference for replacing CVSS developed into a balanced position: CVSS retained value for severity communication, while SSVC supported contextual prioritisation (Spring et al., 2020; Wunder et al., 2024). This demonstrated willingness to revise an argument when alternative evidence revealed limitations.

Preparing the opening section of the quantum-computing debate required an uncertain technical issue to be translated into a clear strategic argument. Explaining the threat and justifying early migration planning strengthened communication with a non-specialist audience and reinforced the need for coherence across individual contributions.

Professional development was evident in a stronger ability to question whether security claims, risk ratings and proposed controls were adequately supported and proportionate to the organisational context. The University of Limerick case demonstrated that phishing and access-control weaknesses cannot be treated solely as technical issues, as inadequate detection, record-keeping and breach-notification processes may also create regulatory and accountability consequences (Data Protection Commission, 2025). Similarly, the disaster recovery activities broadened understanding beyond backup and system restoration to include portability, secure configuration, continuous monitoring, supplier dependency and alignment between operational and security priorities. Security decisions were therefore understood as both technical and governance decisions.

Personal working practices also developed throughout the module. Uncertainty was no longer treated as a weakness to be concealed through apparently precise results, but as a condition that should be acknowledged, documented and communicated transparently. This encouraged earlier planning, greater caution when interpreting evidence and more systematic recording of assumptions and decisions.


### Now What?

Future assessments will begin with documented scope, stakeholders, assets, dependencies and decision purpose. Every likelihood and impact rating will include an evidence source and written justification. For quantitative analysis, each probability range will be justified, at least one alternative scenario will be tested, and priorities will be compared with a qualitative assessment. Model limitations and dependencies will be stated before recommendations are finalised.

Further development will focus on conditional probability, sensitivity analysis and dependency modelling. Numerical outputs will be communicated as ranges rather than precise predictions. An internal deadline will be set at least five days before submission, allowing separate reviews of analysis and evidence, and of referencing, structure and language.

Collaborative work will begin with agreement on methodology, terminology, responsibilities and deadlines. Two integration reviews will be scheduled: one during drafting and one before submission. Shared evidence matrices and version control will reduce inconsistency and clarify individual contributions.

In professional practice, the same approach will support reviews of new digital services by identifying system dependencies, documenting assumptions, assessing data-protection obligations and testing whether recovery arrangements meet operational requirements. Overall, the module developed the ability to approach security and risk management as a continuous, collaborative and evidence-based process rather than as a one-time technical exercise.

## Conclusion

The portfolio demonstrates a shift from identifying isolated technical risks to evaluating interconnected socio-technical systems under uncertainty. The central development was not the adoption of one preferred model, but the ability to select proportionate methods, explain assumptions, integrate peer perspectives and connect controls to business and stakeholder outcomes. The evidence demonstrates increased analytical independence, stronger critical judgement and a more transparent approach to security and risk decision-making.
