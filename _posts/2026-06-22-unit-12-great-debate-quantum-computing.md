## Overview

This post presents evidence of my participation in the **Unit 12 Seminar: The Great Debate – The Future of SRM**. The seminar required each team to choose a trend that could be the most influential in security and risk management over the next five years and prepare a 10-minute presentation arguing why that trend should be selected.

Our group selected **quantum computing** as the most influential trend. The presentation argued that although a quantum computer capable of breaking today’s encryption may still be several years away, the security and risk management impact is already relevant. This is because attackers may collect encrypted data today and decrypt it later once quantum capability becomes available. The presentation also highlighted that migration to quantum-safe encryption is a long-term programme that organisations need to begin before the threat becomes fully realised.

## Key Presentation Points

The presentation made four main arguments. First, quantum computing is relevant now because of the **Harvest Now, Decrypt Later** risk. Encrypted data may be collected today and decrypted in the future when quantum computing becomes powerful enough.

Second, the threat affects many sectors. Government, healthcare, banking, finance and corporate organisations may all hold long-lived sensitive data that must remain confidential for years.

Third, timing matters because migration to quantum-safe encryption is not instant. Organisations need to identify where encryption is used, understand system dependencies and plan a safe transition.

Fourth, security and risk management methods need to adapt. Instead of relying only on precise likelihood calculations, organisations should use scenarios, uncertainty assessment and evidence-based planning when assessing quantum risk.

## Reflection

This seminar activity developed my understanding of how emerging technologies can create security and risk management implications before they become fully mature or widely deployed. At the beginning of the activity, I viewed quantum computing mainly as a future technical development. However, preparing for the debate helped me recognise that its risk relevance is already present, particularly because of long-term confidentiality requirements and the possibility of “Harvest Now, Decrypt Later” attacks. This changed my understanding from seeing quantum computing as a distant technical issue to viewing it as a current strategic risk that requires early planning.

The activity also helped me understand that security and risk management should not focus only on immediate threats. Some risks require preparation long before the full impact is visible. Quantum computing is a useful example because the exact timing of a cryptographically relevant quantum computer remains uncertain, but the consequences for encryption, sensitive data and digital trust could be significant. This showed me that risk assessment should consider uncertainty, long-term impact and the strength of available knowledge, rather than relying only on simple likelihood and impact calculations.

From a collaboration perspective, the activity strengthened my experience of working within a team to prepare a clear and persuasive academic argument. The group had to agree on the selected trend, divide the presentation sections and ensure that the argument remained consistent across the slides. The meeting evidence demonstrates that the preparation involved discussion, coordination and task allocation before the final presentation was completed. This helped me appreciate the importance of planning and communication in group-based academic work.

Overall, this seminar improved my ability to connect technical change with strategic risk management. The main learning point was that future security trends should be assessed not only by when the technology becomes fully available, but also by when organisations need to begin preparing for its impact. In future work, I would apply this learning by considering emerging risks earlier, using scenario-based thinking, and linking technical developments to governance, compliance, business continuity and long-term organisational resilience.
